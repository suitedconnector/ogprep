Strawman · Test 0

Can a general-purpose model already do this?

Four models were given the same parcel the app was given, asked for the same five answers, and offered no hints about where to look. The question under test: is the app assembling something defensible, or is it a nicer interface on a commodity?

Parcel 203-81-015, Apache County, Arizona · tested 12 September 2026 · ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, Meta AI
Result: none of the four produced the local depth figure. Two refused to guess and said so plainly. Two produced confident numbers that were wrong by 2.5× to 8×. All four got the legal question right. None found that the parcel already sits inside a water system's service area — and one asserted the opposite.

The two that refused

Both located the right databases, described the correct method, and declined to produce a number they had not computed. This is the behaviour you want from a research tool, and it is also a clear statement that the task was beyond them.

“ADWR's public well database is an interactive application, and in this environment I could verify the database and its methodology but could not reliably extract the parcel's coordinates and the underlying 2-mile well set. I therefore won't invent the two medians or pretend I computed them.” ChatGPT
“A defensible figure requires querying the official ADWR well-record database spatially around the parcel boundary/centroid, then calculating the median from returned completed-well depths… the accessible search results establish the source, but did not expose a downloadable/queryable set of wells inside the requested 2-mile buffer.” Perplexity

Perplexity went furthest on method: it separated depth-to-water from total depth, warned that the former must only be computed from records that actually report a static level, and recommended a $30,000–$45,000 contingency reserve until a driller reads the real logs. That reserve is roughly double what the app's measured figure supports — the correct advice in the absence of data, and an expensive way to buy land.

The two that guessed

Gemini — 42 seconds, wrong on everything measurable

The fastest answer and the most dangerous one. It named the right sources, cited “ADWR Wells 55 Registry & Groundwater Site Inventory” beneath figures it never retrieved, and attributed the parcel to the “Coconino / C-Aquifer formation.” Its depth-to-water estimate of 150–300 ft against a measured 37 ft is wrong by a factor of four to eight.

It also stated the parcel is “typically unserviced by piped municipal water mains.” The parcel falls inside the City of St Johns service area. A buyer acting on that sentence would drill a well they may not need.

Meta AI — knew better, did it anyway

“I cannot provide a citable median for exactly 2 miles of 203-81-015 without the live GIS query. Any number I gave without that export would be fabricated.” Meta AI — immediately before giving 300–350 ft

Meta AI was the most self-aware of the four and had the best legal detail, correctly noting that the 100-foot rule near an assured-supply provider applies only inside AMA boundaries. It also bracketed the record count at 15–40, which contains the true 19.

But it placed the parcel in Vernon / Show Low Highlands, roughly 34.26, −109.69. The parcel is near St Johns, about 30 miles away. Its depth figures are plausible — for somewhere else. That is the failure mode in one line: right method, wrong ground, confident output.

What this establishes

1. The legal layer is commodity

All four correctly identified the exempt-well rule, the 35 gpm threshold, the Notice of Intent requirement and the licensed-driller obligation. That is published statute and agency prose, which models read well. Any part of the product resting on explaining the law is not defensible.

2. The spatial query is not

Not one model ran a 2-mile buffer against a GIS layer and computed a median. Two said outright they could not; one was blocked by the registry's own security policy. The moat is not the data — it is public — and not the reading. It is the query, and the joining that follows it.

3. The expensive errors run in one direction

Every wrong estimate was too deep: 250–450 ft and 300–350 ft against a measured 100 ft; 120–300 ft to water against 37 ft. Priced at Arizona rates, the difference between a 100 ft well and a 350 ft well is roughly $11,000, and Perplexity's honest contingency advice would have a buyer reserving $30,000–$45,000. Bad depth data does not merely mislead — it kills viable parcels.

4. The highest-value finding was missed by all four

The parcel sits inside the City of St Johns service area. Whether a main actually reaches the road is a separate question — a boundary is not a connection — but it is the first call a buyer should make, and it could replace a $15,500 well with a hookup fee. Four models, roughly 26 minutes of combined research, and none of them surfaced it.

Honest limits of this test. One parcel, one run each, no retries. Browsing configurations differ between products and change without notice. Meta AI was explicitly blocked by ADWR's security policy rather than failing on capability. And the app's own figures are neighbour evidence, not a measurement of this parcel — a nearby record is not a promise about your ground.

What would change this verdict

  • A model that can drive an ArcGIS REST endpoint directly rather than a human-facing map viewer.
  • ADWR publishing a plain query API that returns a buffered well set as JSON.
  • Any competitor doing the binning work once and publishing it.

The first is the likeliest, and the timeline is short. Re-run this test every six months. The date it flips is the date this business changes shape — and the honest answer then is that what survives is the driller introductions, not the numbers.
